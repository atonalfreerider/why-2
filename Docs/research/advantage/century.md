# Century: who won, and how they lost the lead

Cluster `century`, written 7 Nov 2026. All numbers carry fact ids in brackets. The facts are from Wikipedia (history), and from Statcounter for the October 2026 shares. Sources can change; the gate re-read them today.

## How to read it

We walk back from the 2026 winners. For each winner we ask: whom did it displace, and with which advantage? Then we take the great 20th-century winners and ask how each lost its lead. Helmer's powers and the user's triad (information, execution, environment) name the mechanism.

## Part 1: today's winners and the predecessors they displaced

**Google over Yahoo.** Google holds 91.05% of worldwide search; Bing 4.8%; Yahoo! 1.18% [century-google-search-share-2026]. In 1998 Yahoo declined to buy Google for $1 million, and in 2002 Google rejected Yahoo's final offer of $3 billion [century-yahoo-google-offer]. Yahoo's core business went to Verizon for $4.83 billion in 2016 [century-yahoo-verizon-2016], after Microsoft's $44.6 billion bid in 2008 [century-microsoft-yahoo-bid]. Advantage: information (the PageRank patent and query data) plus network economies. Yahoo owned the portal and the brand, and brand did not stop a better product.

**Apple over Nokia and BlackBerry.** Nokia reached 40.4% of handsets in Q4 2007, and 51% of smartphones [century-nokia-2007-share]. BlackBerry had 85 million subscribers in September 2011, 23 million by March 2016 [century-blackberry-subscribers]. In October 2026 Android holds 72.36% and iOS 27.61% of mobile OS use; Windows is at 0% [century-mobile-os-share-2026]. Nokia bet on Windows Phone in February 2011 [century-nokia-windows-phone-2011]. The advantage that won was an ecosystem (switching costs and network effects), not the handset. Apple's route also ran through Xerox: in 1979 Jobs arranged a visit to PARC and Apple saw the GUI [century-xerox-parc-apple-1979]. Apple was the first US public company at $1 trillion, on 2 August 2018 [century-apple-1t-2018].

**Microsoft over IBM.** More than 90% of the 15 million Americans using a PC at work used MS-DOS [century-msdos-90pct]. IBM's own position had been the reverse: it made 80 percent of US computers and 70 percent of world computers in the 1960s and 1970s [century-ibm-1960s-share], then posted an $8 billion loss in 1993 [century-ibm-1993-loss]. IBM's PC used an outside operating system and was easy to clone, so the profit moved to Microsoft. Microsoft in turn was found guilty of monopolisation on 3 April 2000 [century-microsoft-guilty-2000] and ordered broken up on 7 June 2000 [century-microsoft-breakup-ordered-2000], and then missed mobile (the Nokia bet above).

**Amazon in cloud.** AWS had 31% of cloud infrastructure in Q1 2023, Azure 25%, Google Cloud 11% [century-aws-share-2023]. Scale economies and switching costs.

**Meta: buy the challenger.** Facebook bought Instagram for approximately US$1 billion in April 2012 [century-instagram-acquired] and WhatsApp for US$19 billion in February 2014 [century-whatsapp-2014]. Network economies defended by acquisition.

**Netflix over Blockbuster.** Blockbuster had 84,300 employees and 9,094 stores at its 2004 peak [century-blockbuster-2004-peak]. It turned down a chance to buy Netflix for $50 million in 2000 [century-blockbuster-declined-netflix] and filed for Chapter 11 on 23 September 2010 with $900 million of debt [century-blockbuster-2010-bankruptcy]. This is counter-positioning: the new model hurt the old one's store income if copied.

**Walmart over Sears.** Walmart became the largest US retailer by revenue by October 1989 [century-walmart-passes-sears]; Sears filed for Chapter 11 on 15 October 2018 [century-sears-bankruptcy-2018]. As of 2026 Walmart is the second-largest company by revenue and the largest private employer with 2.1 million employees [century-walmart-2026-rank]. Process power in logistics and price.

**Toyota over GM.** GM was the world's largest automaker for 77 years before Toyota took the title in 2008 [century-gm-lost-title-2008]; by March 2009 GM had received $17.4 billion in bailouts [century-gm-bailout-2009]. As of 2024 Toyota was the world's largest car company, with revenues above US$400 billion [century-toyota-2024-largest]. Process power (the Toyota Production System).

**China and Huawei.** China's manufacturing output exceeds the nine next largest manufacturers combined [century-china-manufacturing-nine]. Huawei passed Ericsson in 2012 as the largest telecom equipment maker [century-huawei-passes-ericsson]. Scale and state direction.

## Part 2: the 20th-century winners and how they lost the lead

- **Standard Oil.** 91% of US refining and 85% of final sales in 1904 [century-standard-oil-1904-share]; 64% by 1911, when it was ordered broken up [century-standard-oil-1911-share]. The state ended it, but rivals had already taken a third.
- **U.S. Steel.** Roughly two-thirds of US steel in 1901 [century-us-steel-1901-share]. Scale won; later it became a smaller producer.
- **Ford and GM.** Ford's moving assembly line, 1913 [century-ford-assembly-line-1913], made the car affordable; GM then held first place for 77 years (see Toyota above).
- **AT&T and the Bell System.** One system of long distance, 24 local companies, Western Electric and Bell Labs [century-bell-breakup]; the divestiture took effect on 1 January 1984 [century-bell-divestiture-1984].
- **IBM.** See Microsoft above.
- **Xerox.** PARC invented the interface; Apple commercialised it [century-xerox-parc-apple-1979].
- **Kodak and Fuji.** Fuji's US film share rose from 10% in the early 1990s to 17% in 1997 by price cutting [century-fuji-kodak-share]; Kodak filed for Chapter 11 in January 2012 [century-kodak-2012-chapter11].
- **Japan Inc.** The Nikkei touched 38,957.44 on 29 December 1989 and had halved by August 1990 [century-nikkei-1989-peak].
- **GE.** The most valuable company in the world in August 2000 at $601 billion [century-ge-2000-market-cap]; removed from the Dow on 28 June 2018 [century-ge-removed-dow-2018].
- **Enron and Lehman.** Enron claimed nearly $101 billion of revenue in 2000 [century-enron-2000-revenue] and went bankrupt on 2 December 2001. Lehman filed on 15 September 2008 with assets worth $639 billion [century-lehman-assets].

## The repeating patterns of decline

1. **The incumbent protects its profit pool.** Blockbuster's late fees, Kodak's film, Sears's real-estate and finance business. The new entrant's model hurts the old one if copied (counter-positioning).
2. **Outsourcing the part that matters.** IBM used outside parts and an outside operating system; the profit left with them.
3. **Ecosystem beats product.** Nokia's handset share did not matter once iOS and Android held the developers.
4. **Declining a cheap option on the challenger.** Yahoo said no to Google; Blockbuster said no to Netflix. The cost of the option was small against the later loss.
5. **Process beats scale.** Toyota's lower defect rates beat GM's size; Walmart's logistics beat Sears.
6. **The state is the final referee.** Standard Oil, AT&T, Microsoft: antitrust ended or constrained monopolies; GM was kept alive with a bailout. The same state supplies the IP protection that created them.
7. **Leverage and opacity.** Enron, Lehman and GE's finance arm: the numbers looked strong until funding left.
8. **Buying the threat.** Meta bought Instagram and WhatsApp; Microsoft bid for Yahoo. The strategy works until the regulator acts.

## What a strategist would predict

- Dominance is a position in a market, not a trait of the firm. It ends when the profit pool moves, when a rival's model cannot be copied without damage to the old one, when the state intervenes, or when funding leaves.
- Today's choke points (Google search at 91.05%, AWS at 31% of cloud, Apple's iOS at 27.61% of mobile with the profit) are exposed to the same pattern: a model that cannibalises the incumbent (AI answers for search), an antitrust remedy, or a shift of platform.
- Firms with outside dependencies (IBM's PC, Nokia's software) lose to the firm that owns the standard. Apply that to any firm now buying its key input from a single supplier.
- Exogenous environments: antitrust is more severe for monopolies on information; a credit shock exposes leveraged firms (Lehman, GE); a currency or capital shock ended Japan's lead.
